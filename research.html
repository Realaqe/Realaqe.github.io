Research

I measure the masses and orbits of giant planets and brown dwarfs. Most substellar companions are seen only indirectly, as a wobble in their host star's radial velocity or position on the sky, and each technique alone leaves the mass or orbital orientation uncertain. By fitting Hipparcos and Gaia astrometry, radial velocities, and direct imaging together, I obtain model-independent dynamical masses and full three-dimensional orbits, and use them to study how these objects form and evolve.

Orbits and masses from Hipparcos–Gaia astrometry

Hipparcos measured about 100,000 bright stars around 1991, and Gaia measured them again around 2016. Comparing the proper motions from each mission with the long-term proper motion over the 25-year gap reveals the slow acceleration that a long-period companion induces on its host star. Combined with radial velocities, this breaks the mass–inclination degeneracy of radial velocities alone. Using the open-source code orvara, I fit orbits and dynamical masses for 156 companions (planets, brown dwarfs, and stars) in a uniform way. The catalog clearly shows the brown-dwarf desert, and it is the reference sample I use to test Gaia's own orbital solutions.

  • An, Q., Brandt, T. D., Brandt, G. M., & Venner, A. 2025, ApJS, 280, 61 — Orbits and Masses for 156 Companions from Combined Astrometry and Radial Velocities, and a Validation of Gaia Non-single-star Solutions
  • Venner, A., An, Q., et al. 2024, MNRAS, 535, 90 — HD 28185 Revisited: An Outer Planet, Instead of a Brown Dwarf, on a Saturn-like Orbit

Three-dimensional architectures of planetary systems

Knowing a companion's full orbital orientation lets us ask how a system is tilted, not just how massive its planets are. In HAT-P-11, I combined astrometry and radial velocities with the stellar spin to show that the orbits of both planets are significantly inclined, relative to each other and to the star. This was the first spin–orbit angle measured for a non-transiting outer giant planet. In a companion paper, we traced this geometry to an early history of planet–planet scattering followed by von Zeipel–Lidov–Kozai migration.

  • An, Q., Lu, T., Brandt, G. M., Brandt, T. D., & Li, G. 2025, AJ, 169, 22 — Significant Mutual Inclinations Between the Stellar Spin and the Orbits of Both Planets in the HAT-P-11 System
  • Lu, T., An, Q., Li, G., et al. 2025, ApJ, 979, 218 — Planet–Planet Scattering and von Zeipel–Lidov–Kozai Migration—The Dynamical History of HAT-P-11

Gaia-only accelerations: orbits for stars Hipparcos never saw

Most Gaia stars, including nearly all nearby M dwarfs, were too faint for Hipparcos, so the Hipparcos–Gaia method cannot reach them. For many of these stars, Gaia DR3 fits an acceleration of its own. A 7-parameter solution adds an acceleration to the usual five astrometric parameters, and a 9-parameter solution also adds a jerk. I extended orvara to fit these solutions directly, together with radial velocities and imaging, while converting Gaia's photocenter motion into the motion of the host star.

Can these solutions be trusted? Testing them against systems whose orbits we already know, I find that Gaia's accelerations agree in direction and size but scatter by about twice their quoted errors. Orbital solutions near the long-period edge of Gaia's period grid can also be spurious. A second test, which matches every DR3 acceleration solution against resolved pairs in the Washington Double Star catalog, checks whether Gaia's accelerations point toward known companions (in prep).

The framework already makes discoveries. By combining Subaru/IRD radial velocities, a Keck image, and the Gaia acceleration, we measured a 60 Jupiter-mass brown dwarf orbiting the M dwarf LSPM J1446+4633 at 4.3 au, with a mass precise to about 1.5 Jupiter masses (JHU news). Brown dwarfs around such low-mass stars sit inside the brown-dwarf desert in mass but far above it in mass ratio, so they help show whether the desert is set by the companion's mass or by its mass relative to the star. Gaia DR4 will provide epoch astrometry for every star. Rather than simply adopting Gaia's solutions, I plan to refit them within this framework.

  • An, Q., Brandt, T. D., Brandt, G. M., & Venner, A. 2025, ApJS, 280, 61 — validation of Gaia DR3 acceleration and orbital solutions (Known Orbits Test)

Software

  • orvara — Orbit fitting that jointly models radial velocities, relative astrometry, and Hipparcos–Gaia absolute astrometry. I am a co-developer and maintainer.
  • htof — Reproduces Hipparcos and Gaia intermediate astrometric data so that orbit fits can use them. I am a co-developer and maintainer.
